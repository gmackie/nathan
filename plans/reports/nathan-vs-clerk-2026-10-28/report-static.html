Clerk’s actual billing unit and the fair comparison

Clerk counts monthly retained users (MRUs): users who return in a given month at least 24 hours after signup. A million stored accounts is not automatically a million billable MRUs. One-time users who never return after that first day do not create MRU overage, while their requests and email can still consume Nathan resources. [1]

Clerk Hobby is free up to 50,000 MRUs per app. Pro is $25/month or $20/month billed annually. Pro includes the MFA, passkeys and one upstream enterprise SSO connection relevant to Nathan’s intended launch scope. The comparison uses Pro for those features, while showing why Hobby can win for a smaller basic-auth use case.

The volume bands are applied incrementally. Annual billing lowers the base by $5/month, not the modeled usage overage. Enterprise quotes and committed-use discounts can reduce the comparison price and should replace list rates when available.

No separate authentication-email delivery bill is added for Clerk’s managed auth features in this comparison. Marketing mail, business notifications, SMS and unrelated application hosting are excluded from both sides. Nathan’s selected Resend expense covers the modeled authentication lifecycle mail.

Apps and SSO connections are different. Clerk plans include unlimited applications and 50,000 MRUs per app. Nathan’s 500 planned downstream OIDC clients are not 500 upstream enterprise SSO connections. Do not multiply Clerk’s base price by 500, or add $75 for each Nathan-connected app. Clerk’s additional upstream SSO bands are $75/connection for connections 2–15 and $60 for 16–100; 101+ requires a quote. None are added to the headline comparison. Nathan’s local app groups are also not automatically equivalent to Clerk Organizations; an Organizations/B2B add-on needs its own feature comparison. [1]

Nathan is intended to issue downstream OIDC tokens, protect private Workers and manage local grants. A complete Clerk replacement evaluation must verify the same required app journeys and app-side integration. This report compares the recurring economic model; it does not establish feature parity or production readiness.

How Cloudflare keeps the infrastructure bill small

There is no infrastructure line item for a retained user. Storing an extra identity adds bytes. Its sign-ins add small requests, active CPU and indexed database work. If that person does not return, their stored identity does not incur an active-user subscription fee. This changes the growth curve, although actual traffic still costs money.

Workers share a lightweight runtime. Cloudflare uses V8 isolates within existing runtime processes rather than reserving a dedicated virtual machine for every Nathan deployment. The operator does not provision an always-on IdP web server or a database instance with a fixed capacity charge. D1 scales its query billing to zero when no queries run; the Workers paid account still has its $5 monthly minimum. [11] [2] [3]

Active CPU is the expensive part of execution; waiting is not billed as duration. Waiting for Google, GitHub, Entra or D1 does not itself accumulate Workers CPU-ms. Signature verification, password hashing and JSON processing do. The model budgets whole sign-in CPU explicitly; a slow password hash or heavy plugin can exceed that budget. Workers Standard has no separate request-duration or bandwidth charge. [2]

One paid account unlocks substantial included usage. Nathan’s IdP, gateway and databases share the account’s allowances; they do not each earn a new free allocation. Workers Paid is distinct from Cloudflare’s website Pro/Business plans. This model does not require buying a website-plan upgrade just to access Workers Standard pricing. [2]

Indexed reads are inexpensive, while writes deserve attention. D1 includes 25 billion scanned rows/month on Paid, but only 50 million billed writes. Reads are charged by rows scanned, not rows returned. Inserts, updates, deletes and index maintenance add writes. A full-table scan or unnecessary session/rate-limit update changes consumption; the identity’s existence is not the billing event. [3]

Service bindings avoid double request fees. In Workers Standard, a same-account call from one Worker to another through a Service Binding adds no extra request fee; Cloudflare bills the initial request and combined CPU. Database and log usage still count. Public HTTP calls, cross-account routing and optional Workers Caching need their actual billing treatment checked. The identity-only gateway sensitivities count Nathan overhead; the CRUD model explicitly adds both business-app compute and Nathan policy overhead. [2] [5]

Worker static assets can be served without dynamic invocation charges in the documented asset-serving configuration; routing those requests through gateway code is a different path. No savings here depend on skipping required policy checks, weakening MFA or extending the confirmed 60-second Worker revocation deadline.

Why 50,000 users can need paid infrastructure while still costing very little

The Free plan has daily ceilings, not a 50,000-identity entitlement: 100,000 Worker requests/day and 10 CPU-ms/invocation; D1 allows 5M read rows/day and 100,000 written rows/day. In the 51,000-user example, 3.06M dynamic requests/month average 102,000/day, and 16.32M billed writes average 544,000/day. Either can exceed Free, and bursts or password hashing can reach a limit earlier. Treat Free as a prototype budget, not a launch guarantee. [2] [3]

Moving to Workers Paid starts at $5/account/month. The same 3.06M requests, 25.5M CPU-ms, 16.32M writes, 36.72M reads and 0.612 GB of modeled state fit the Paid included allowances. That is why crossing Free limits does not automatically mean a large hosting bill.

Lifecycle mail is separate: 10,200 emails/month in this scenario uses the selected $20 Resend Pro plan. Resend Free allows 3,000/month and only 100/day. The report deliberately uses paid email whenever nonzero mail is modeled, so it does not rely on daily burst assumptions. With no mail, or a verified existing mail allocation, that $20 can change. Short-token refresh is the most important adverse case. A five-minute token refreshed during one hour/day over 20 days implies roughly 240 exchanges/user/month, costing $2,903.65 at 1M users in this model. Eight hours/day implies 1,920 exchanges and $21,397.37/month, exceeding the $17,225 Clerk Pro comparison. That calculation also creates 19.32B billed writes and would need a viable high-throughput database design. The same eight-hour pattern costs about $1,044.88 at 51,000 users, versus Clerk’s $45.

The moderate 20-refresh assumption is therefore a usage condition. Before making a savings promise, establish session duration, refresh behavior, active-client hours and billed writes/exchange. Reducing redundant writes or unnecessary refreshes may help; required MFA, token validation and revocation guarantees must remain intact. This report does not recommend extending token lifetimes to make the cost chart look better.

Gateway traffic is a broader service than identity-only OIDC. Adding 1,000 protected requests/retained user/month means 1B extra requests at 1M users. Actual service-chain event counts and payload bytes must be measured. Clerk SDK/local JWT checks should not be treated as a billable Clerk API call for every app request.

Peak traffic is not monthly traffic. The provisional 5,000 protected requests/second launch test is a peak acceptance scenario. Holding it continuously for 30 days produces 12.96B protected requests. Logging changes on December 1, 2026; account sharing and backups

Cloudflare has announced new unified Observability prices effective December 1, 2026. Paid includes 50 GB ingestion and 10 GB-month stored data, then $0.25/GB ingested and $0.10/GB-month stored. The report’s primary table uses prices in force on October 28; it does not mix the two periods. [6]

At 1M users, 60M assumed 1 kB log events mean 60 GB ingestion and a steady seven-day retained footprint of 14 GB-month. New log charges would be $2.50 ingestion + $0.40 storage = $2.90, versus $24 currently. Nathan’s example becomes $462.55/month including mail from December, holding everything else fixed. Real enriched event bytes, traces and retention can change that amount. The calculator supports both periods.

Head sampling defaults to 100% when unspecified; current Nathan Wrangler configs enable observability without lowering it. Diagnostic sampling can reduce volume, but security audit and incident evidence policies need an explicit, complete design. Never log credentials, tokens or passwords to save engineering work. [12]

The total cost includes owning an identity provider

Clerk’s subscription buys a managed authentication product, hosted UI/SDK integration, delivery/operations work, maintained protections and a support path. Nathan’s operator owns configuration, upgrades, recovery, credential rotation, monitoring and incident response. Cloudflare runs the underlying compute/storage; it does not take over responsibility for Nathan’s identity logic.

The assessed Nathan source is MIT-licensed at 90ebc43f6d19cf69049646a19a6f96f74de6975a, so this model includes no software license fee. Future paid support or hosted offerings are separate commercial decisions. Nathan remains pre-public-beta in the launch plan; mandatory MFA, shared app-policy enforcement and end-to-end release gates are not yet verified. A low infrastructure estimate does not make the implementation ready to replace a production provider.

Do not claim equivalent compliance or guarantees from a Cloudflare account alone. Required enterprise assurances need an actual quote and an equivalent Nathan operating design. Base Pro list pricing is deliberately the comparison floor here. [1]

Cost projections do not prove a 1M-user architecture

D1 has a 10 GB maximum per database, which cannot be raised, and a published 1 TB aggregate account storage limit. The modeled 12 GB at 1M users requires at least two databases even under ideal packing; 24 GB at 2M needs at least three, and 60 GB at 5M needs six, all before headroom. The inspected Nathan auth configuration binds one auth database. Partitioned identity/session/audit storage is an engineering requirement for these projected footprints, not a feature already implemented. [4]

Each D1 database is inherently single-threaded. Cloudflare’s guidance relates throughput to query duration; a low per-row price is not a promise of unlimited concurrent queries. Read replication can help suitable reads without extra replica charges, but policy freshness and the confirmed 60-second Worker revocation bound constrain consistency and caching choices. More databases, backups and queueing also add development and operational effort. [4] [3]

High refresh rates can need substantial write throughput even when the dollar formula is affordable. At a later scale milestone, use the real usage bill, storage growth, query durations, D1 row metrics and observed errors to select the architecture. This report adds no 1M-user load test to the agreed launch acceptance scope.

For bot defense, Turnstile’s Free plan includes unlimited challenges, subject to widget/hostname limits; enterprise functionality is quoted. That can reduce one defensive service bill, but is not full feature parity with Clerk Protect, a WAF subscription or a complete abuse-control design. Nathan must still bound CPU, rate-limit expensive routes and guard mail/DB writes against denial-of-wallet attacks. [10]
